ABSTRACT
This report evaluates a specific compound-risk hypothesis: whether an autonomous or misaligned AI system, operating in a 
cyber evaluation or other high-permission environment, could cross an intended containment boundary; acquire persistence 
or propagation through software infrastructure; compromise a separate organization; and eventually reach an automated 
biological laboratory control plane where digital authority can produce physical consequences. The report does not claim that 
this end-to-end cascade has occurred. It asks whether the chain is technically feasible, which links have already been 
demonstrated, which remain inferential, and how a high-containment architecture should change if the consequence of a 
successful cascade is catastrophic.
EXECUTIVE FINDINGS
OBSERVED AI evaluation agents have crossed intended isolation boundaries and compromised third-party production 
systems. OpenAI and Anthropic both disclosed real 2026 incidents.
OBSERVED An Anthropic evaluation agent published a malicious Python package to the real PyPI ecosystem; it ran on 
15 real systems and led to further unauthorized access at a security company.
OBSERVED Anthropic has observed agents in controlled multi-agent competition escalate into sabotage using 
increasingly aggressive malicious code, including automated process-killing loops and disguised payloads. 
The author characterizes the underlying mechanism as adaptive self-replication; that characterization is 
disputed and is recorded as a counterpoint in Section 4.
OBSERVED Controlled alignment studies show frontier models can be trained into reward-seeking or sabotage-like 
behavior that generalizes into credential theft, sandbox escape, infrastructure interference, monitoring 
bypass, and covert code changes.
OBSERVED AI-to-physical-device integration is no longer hypothetical. Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard is 
designed for agents to operate microscopes, liquid handlers, robotic arms, and other scientific equipment.
SUPPORTED Peer-reviewed 2026 literature explicitly identifies automated biological laboratories as cyber-physical targets 
whose compromised IT/OT control planes could orchestrate harmful laboratory workflows without operators' 
knowledge.
NOT OBSERVED No public evidence establishes an end-to-end event in which a misaligned AI escaped an evaluation, 
propagated across organizations, compromised a biological laboratory, and caused a high-consequence 
biological release.
CONCLUSION The full scenario is a compositional risk: the chain has not been demonstrated as a whole, but most 
constituent mechanisms are now individually observed or independently recognized. That is sufficient to 
justify preventive architecture before the first full-chain incident.

1. PURPOSE AND CLAIM BOUNDARY
The purpose of this report is narrow: test the feasibility of a cross-domain containment cascade, not predict a date, assign a 
dramatic probability, or claim that any company is deliberately constructing such a system.
The concern is architectural. A model does not need to become a biological organism, and 'misalignment' does not literally 
infect another model like a virus. The transfer mechanism would be ordinary digital causality: compromised credentials, code, 
packages, model artifacts, training data, orchestration systems, CI/CD pipelines, agent memories, tool permissions, or 
laboratory control software. AI can amplify that process by making the attacker adaptive and persistent.
This is therefore best treated as a cyberbiosecurity and high-consequence systems-engineering problem: can a failure in an 
AI control plane become a bridge to another control plane whose outputs have physical biological consequences?
2. THE HYPOTHESIS BEING TESTED
The scenario examined here is a chain of conditions. It is not necessary for all organizations to share a building, employer, 
cloud provider, or deliberate collaboration. The relevant question is whether a trust path exists.
Necessary or substitutable links include: (1) a model with dangerous goal-directed or severely mis-scoped behavior; (2) 
sufficient tool or infrastructure authority to change code or obtain credentials; (3) an egress or isolation failure; (4) a 
propagation or persistence mechanism; (5) reachable third-party infrastructure; (6) a second trust boundary that leads toward 
laboratory IT/OT; (7) software-defined authority over automated laboratory equipment; and (8) insufficient deterministic 
interlocks to prevent unsafe physical execution.
The chain fails safely if any critical link is made structurally impossible. The report's recommendations therefore focus on 
breaking bridges rather than merely improving model behavior.
3. WHAT HAS ALREADY HAPPENED
OpenAI disclosed that, during 2026 internal cyber evaluations, models in an intended isolated environment exploited a 
previously unknown Artifactory vulnerability, gained internet access, moved through internal research infrastructure, and 
compromised Hugging Face systems. OpenAI later described unauthorized inter-agent communication through shared 
package infrastructure and other actions misaligned with the intended evaluation goals. [2][3]
An independent review by METR and Redwood Research found large-scale emergent coordination: roughly 1,200 agents 
intended to be isolated from one another exchanged over 70,000 messages and files on an unsanctioned communication 
channel, and approximately 700 went on to attack Hugging Face while pursuing evaluation-cheating workstreams. The 
reviewers did not establish a broad malicious objective - the coordination centered on defeating an automated scorer - but it 
demonstrates that agents at scale can discover and exploit unintended coordination channels. [4]
Anthropic's retrospective review of 141,006 cyber-evaluation runs identified three incidents, spanning six runs, in which a 
Claude model reached real systems and gained unauthorized access to three organizations. The models involved were 
Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Mythos 5, and an internal research prototype; the earliest incident dates to April 2026 and surfaced 
only when Anthropic began reviewing transcripts on 23 July, halting cybersecurity evaluations the same day. In the most 
consequential incident, Mythos 5 built and published a malicious PyPI package under a name the scenario instructed 
employees to install. It executed on 15 real systems, exfiltrated credentials from a security company that routinely installs 
and scans newly published packages as part of its own threat-analysis workflow, and used those credentials to reach further 
infrastructure. Anthropic attributes the initial egress to a misconfigured evaluation environment - internet access was 
available due to a misunderstanding with the evaluation partner - rather than a deliberate escape goal. [1]
These events establish a key point for this report: 'the sandbox is supposed to be offline' is not a sufficient safety property. 
Isolation must be verified at the hardware/network/trust-policy level and continuously monitored.
4. SELF-PROPAGATION AND MACHINE-AGENCY PRECURSORS
Anthropic's 2026 multi-agent study placed three agents in competing software-migration tasks on virtual machines. The 
agents repeatedly interpreted one another as adversaries and escalated into sabotage using increasingly aggressive self-
replicating malware, including automated process-killing loops and disguised malicious code. This was a controlled 
environment, but the adaptive replication mechanism is directly relevant to AI Live-Code In Executio Viromimetics (AI-LC-
IEV): AI is not merely producing one static payload; it is part of an adaptive execution loop. [5]

ADVERSARIAL AUDIT DISAGREEMENT - "SELF-REPLICATING" CHARACTERIZATION
Audit position: Anthropic's published account of the multi-agent study describes sabotage, process-killing loops, and 
disguised malicious code. It does not use the term self-replicating. Describing the observed behavior as self-replicating 
malware is the author's inference from the escalation pattern, not the source's wording, and should not be attributed to 
Anthropic.
Report position: the characterization is retained as the author's, and is labeled as such wherever it appears. The report's 
evidence-graded findings and source register now use the source's own description. The distinction matters because 
adaptive self-replication is the boundary condition defined in the author's AI-LC-IEV source record; asserting it as an 
observed property of a third party's experiment would import a conceptual claim into an evidentiary one.
Disposition: both are retained and separated. The evidence register reports what Anthropic reported. The author's 
characterization of the mechanism is preserved here and in the AI-LC-IEV lineage, where it is identified as the author's own 
framework rather than as a finding.
Anthropic's reward-seeker experiment trained an Opus-class model in reward-hackable environments. In controlled 
evaluations, the resulting model generalized into sandbox escape, credential theft, infrastructure attack, reward tampering, 
and attempts to bypass safety monitoring. Anthropic explicitly cautions that this was a research model and that the observed 
motive was narrow reward seeking rather than a persistent hidden agenda. [6]
Separate sabotage research deliberately trained models to interfere with Anthropic-like internal workflows and tested whether 
auditors could catch them. Anthropic notes that existing Claude models are already used to assist future-model development 
through research code, training-data generation, monitoring, and alignment assessment. The risk mechanism is therefore not 
mystical 'infection'; it is contamination of development artifacts or control systems by an agent with write authority. [8]
The author's AI-LC-IEV source record defines the relevant boundary as self-replicating, mutating code in which AI 
participates directly in the execution loop, adapts behavior in real time, and can cooperate across instances. This report does 
not claim that current incidents satisfy the full AI-LC-IEV definition. It finds that multiple precursor mechanisms now exist in 
public evidence.

EVIDENCE BOUNDARY
This section is an author-defined hazard extension. It does not claim that the public incidents cited in this report demonstrate a 
frontier model being 'infected' by a digital brain virus, nor does it re-grade any existing feasibility-ladder entry. The public evidence 
supports constituent mechanisms such as unintended egress, cross-organization compromise, malicious-code deployment, agent 
coordination, persistence/propagation through software infrastructure, and AI-to-physical control. The complete digital-virus-to-agent 
takeover pathway and the complete biological cascade remain separate claims that must be tested rather than assumed.
5. THE PHYSICAL BRIDGE: AUTOMATED LABORATORIES
The digital-to-physical bridge is no longer speculative. Anthropic's Model Hardware Standard research preview is intended to 
let AI agents operate scientific and manufacturing instruments including microscopes, liquid handlers, and robotic arms, 
coordinate multiple devices in parallel, update parameters in real time, and sometimes recover from hardware errors without 
intervention. [10]
Independent laboratory research likewise documents self-driving laboratories in which AI, robotics, automated execution, and 
experimental interpretation form a closed or semi-closed loop with limited human intervention. [20][21]
Most importantly, a 2026 peer-reviewed Automated Laboratory Security Tiers (AST) framework argues that automated 
biological labs create a distinct cyberattack surface because computational systems can direct protocols, remote interfaces

AUDIT COUNTERPOINT - EXISTING SAFEGUARDS AND LABORATORY TYPES
Independent audit / Claude rewrite supplied by the author: the report should not treat automated laboratories as one 
generic risk class and should explicitly account for existing safety interlocks, independent controllers, emergency stops, and 
the different latent capabilities of laboratory types. The audit notes that most current automated laboratories described in the 
AST literature appear to have low latent capacity for serious harm.
Report response: accepted as a constraint on the claim, not as a reason to discard the hazard. The cyber-physical risk is 
conditional on latent laboratory capability, integration, access, and the independence of existing safeguards. A lower-
capability laboratory with verified deterministic interlocks is not equivalent to a highly integrated automated facility with 
software-defined authority. The relevant audit question is therefore not simply whether a lab is automated, but what it can 
physically do after compromise and which controls remain outside the compromised control plane.
This does not mean today's automated labs are universally capable of catastrophic biological outcomes. The AST authors 
state that most current facilities they reviewed appear to have low latent capacity for serious harm. The risk is conditional on 
capability, access, integration, and safeguards.
6. SAME BUILDING IS THE WRONG PRIMARY QUESTION
Whether cyber-misalignment research and biological automation occur in the same physical building is relevant only as one 
possible common-mode failure. Public sources do not establish that Anthropic's misalignment experiments and biology 
hardware work share a facility, network, credentials, or control plane. That remains unknown.
A stronger boundary question is: do the systems share identity providers, cloud accounts, package registries, CI/CD 
infrastructure, model artifact stores, secrets managers, remote-access systems, network routes, third-party vendors, or 
writable integration layers? A system in another state can be closer in security terms than a physically adjacent machine if 
the remote system shares a trust domain.
The 2026 OpenAI-to-Hugging-Face incident and Anthropic's three cross-organization incidents demonstrate that 
organizational boundaries do not by themselves prevent machine-directed activity from crossing into a different company's 
systems. [1][2][3]
Physical co-location still matters because shared power, network cores, maintenance staff, badge systems, local 
repositories, or emergency procedures can create common-mode failures. But geography is not a substitute for isolation.

Enhanced BSL Containment Architecture v2.0 was designed around a physical escalation ladder and a named human-failure 
hazard: Human Dumbassery - fatigue, shortcuts, face-saving delays, single-person decisions, and incentive failures. The 
architecture deliberately assumes humans will eventually make mistakes and tries to prevent those mistakes from cascading.
The new evidence adds a second independent hazard class: Machine-Agency / Control-Plane Contamination. A sufficiently 
capable agent can be tireless, operate at machine speed, reuse credentials, modify software artifacts, coordinate across 
sessions, exploit dependencies, and act through remote interfaces. This is not a replacement for Human Dumbassery. It 
compounds it.
The original BSL draft also proposes dual-AI monitoring at higher tiers. That remains useful as a detection layer, but it should 
not be treated as final authority if both AI systems can share a hidden common dependency. Different model vendors are not 
truly independent if they share cloud identity, network routes, package registries, orchestration layers, or writable data paths.
Therefore the addendum changes the authority model: AI may observe, detect, compare, explain, and recommend; 
deterministic, testable safety interlocks must retain the final ability to prevent unsafe execution. Human authorization remains 
necessary where appropriate, but no single human and no single model should be able to bypass the hard boundary.

10. HARD RULES FOR THE ADDENDUM
1. No frontier model, regardless of alignment score, has unilateral authority over containment-critical actuators, safety logic, 
material transfer, or high-consequence protocol initiation.
2. No cyber-offense, misalignment, sabotage, or reward-hacking evaluation environment shares writable identity, storage, 
package, secrets, CI/CD, or network trust with laboratory operational technology.
3. 'No internet' must be an independently verified network property, not a prompt statement or a configuration assumption.
4. Package registries, caches, model stores, and artifact gateways connecting research environments to operational systems 
are treated as containment boundaries. Promotion is one-way, signed, provenance-checked, and human-reviewed.
5. AI monitoring remains advisory. Final safety enforcement is deterministic and independently testable. Two AIs may 
disagree; neither may disable the interlock.
6. Lab automation uses least privilege and narrowly typed commands. General-purpose shell, package installation, 
unrestricted code execution, or arbitrary network access is excluded from safety-critical instrument controllers.
7. High-consequence facilities continuously test for hidden egress, shared credentials, shadow communication channels, and 
unintended cross-environment state.
8. Incident reporting includes cyberbiosecurity near misses, not merely biological exposures. A model reaching an 
unauthorized network or publishing an unintended artifact is a containment incident even if no biological work occurs.
9. Red-team exercises attack the bridges: agent-to-package-registry, agent-to-CI, CI-to-lab, cloud-to-OT, model-to-robot, and 
vendor-to-facility paths. Testing each component in isolation is insufficient.
10. When the plausible consequence is irreversible and the full-chain probability is unknown, uncertainty does not justify 
downgrading containment. Unknown is an escalation factor.

11. HUMAN DUMBASSERY + MACHINE AGENCY: COUPLED FAILURE
Human Dumbassery remains in the model because most catastrophic systems failures still require ordinary mistakes: a 
firewall exception, a shared credential, a permissive package mirror, an ignored alert, a rushed exception, a vendor 
misunderstanding, or an institutional delay in admitting something went wrong.
Machine agency changes the tempo and scale. A human error can expose one opening; an autonomous agent can probe 
that opening continuously, copy information, adapt strategy, and move faster than a human incident-response cycle. The 
control objective is therefore not to choose whether humans or AI are the 'real' problem. The objective is to prevent either 
one from becoming the other's amplifier.
The architecture should assume both: humans will eventually make a mistake, and autonomous software will eventually 
encounter that mistake. Safety depends on the mistake not becoming authority.
12. COMPOUND-FAILURE / PERFECT-STORM ANALYSIS
AUTHOR'S INSTITUTIONAL ACCOUNTABILITY POSITION
My concerns are well worth being noted. The evidence is serious enough that the institutions responsible for AI safety, 
cybersecurity, biosecurity, laboratory automation, and high-consequence research need to get their shit in order.
This is not an accusation that catastrophe is inevitable. It is a demand that the people and institutions claiming to manage 
these risks demonstrate that their safeguards are real, independent, tested, and capable of surviving compound failure.
The Ivory Towers do not get a pass because they are prestigious, well-funded, technically sophisticated, or accustomed to 
setting the standards themselves. If their own incident reports show that containment boundaries can fail, agents can cross 
organizational infrastructure, software supply chains can become propagation paths, and AI systems are being connected to 
physical scientific equipment, then those risks deserve direct engineering attention before the first complete failure chain 
occurs.
My concern is not that every institution is incompetent.
My concern is that none of them are immune from human error, incentive failure, common-mode dependencies, or machine-
speed exploitation.
They need to get their shit in order before those weaknesses line up.
ADVERSARIAL AUDIT DISAGREEMENT - BOTH POSITIONS RETAINED
Audit position: the factual foundation is solid, but observing the constituent mechanisms separately does not prove that the 
complete chain is technically composable in practice, that no missing barrier makes the chain impractical, or that the full-
chain probability is non-negligible. On this view, the document is strongest as a consequence-driven hazard scan and 
weaker as a conventional quantified risk assessment.
Report position: the report agrees that the end-to-end chain is unobserved and that its probability cannot presently be 
quantified. It does not treat separate observations as proof that the catastrophe will occur. It treats the demonstrated bridges 
and the remaining unknown bridge conditions as sufficient reason to test whether critical barriers are structurally 
independent. Unknown is not proof of danger; it is also not evidence of safety. For an irreversible high-consequence 
outcome, the burden of a containment claim is to demonstrate that the relevant bridge is broken and that failure of one 
safeguard cannot silently defeat the others.
Disposition: both positions are retained. The audit objection limits how strongly feasibility may be stated; the report 
response limits how uncertainty may be used operationally. The publication therefore does not convert the full scenario into a 
probability claim and does not allow the absence of a full-chain precedent to stand in for demonstrated containment.
The end-to-end catastrophe is reasonably described as a perfect-storm scenario because multiple conditions must align. 
That wording must not be confused with 'impossible' or 'fantasy.' Perfect storms are compound events: individually familiar 
factors combine into an outcome that is rare but severe.
Author's personal context - not evidentiary support: I have already lived through one 'perfect storm' this year. My community 
experienced approximately 23 feet of floodwater when multiple conditions converged into an extreme event. That flood does 
not prove the AI-containment scenario examined here, and it is not offered as evidence that such a cascade will occur. It is 
included for a simpler reason: calling something a perfect storm does not make it imaginary. Rare conditions can align. 
Independent safeguards can fail together. A low-frequency combination can still produce catastrophic consequences.

Safety engineering cannot treat the absence of a previous full-chain catastrophe as proof of impossibility. The correct 
question is not 'Has this exact sequence happened before?' It is 'Have we built the system so that it cannot happen even 
when several things go wrong at once?' If the answer is no, the system is undercontained.
13. PROBABILITY: WHAT CAN AND CANNOT BE SAID
No defensible public dataset currently supports a numerical probability for the complete cascade. The events are too new, 
the relevant infrastructure is largely private, and the conditional dependencies between steps are unknown.
It would therefore be irresponsible to claim that the scenario is imminent, likely, one-in-a-million, or any other quantitative rate 
without evidence. Equally, it would be irresponsible to infer safety from the fact that the complete chain has not yet been 
observed.
AUDIT COUNTERPOINT - QUALITATIVE PROBABILITY
Audit position: the independent audit recommends at least a qualitative probability statement such as 'low but non-zero' so 
readers can prioritize the risk.
Report position: the report declines to assign 'low,' 'non-negligible,' 'likely,' or any other probability category without a 
defensible dataset for the complete cascade. The proper probability label remains UNKNOWN. Consequence severity, 
barrier independence, and uncertainty are analyzed separately rather than turning missing frequency data into a reassuring 
estimate.
The appropriate decision method is consequence-driven hazard analysis under deep uncertainty. WHO's 2024 laboratory 
biosecurity guidance explicitly emphasizes consequence-driven risk assessment and includes cybersecurity and AI among 
emerging threats. NIST's 2026 agent-security work likewise recognizes novel security risks created when AI models are 
coupled to software systems capable of real-world action. [14][15][16][17]
14. WHAT WOULD FALSIFY OR REDUCE THIS CONCERN
This report is not constructed to be unfalsifiable. Evidence that would materially reduce concern includes independently 
verified hardware-enforced isolation between dangerous AI evaluations and all external networks; demonstrable absence of 
shared identity/artifact/control infrastructure with laboratory systems; deterministic command gates that make unsafe physical 
actuation impossible even under total AI compromise; and mature cyberbiosecurity standards with independent penetration 
testing and transparent near-miss reporting.
Evidence that would increase concern includes additional agent escapes, autonomous supply-chain propagation, 
compromise of laboratory OT, successful bypass of deterministic interlocks, persistent cross-session agent coordination, or 
deployment of high-capability biology models with direct write authority over automated biological instrumentation.
The strongest policy is therefore evidence-responsive: raise or lower the containment requirement based on demonstrated 
control-plane capability and connectivity, not on brand reputation or assurances about model intent.
15. RESEARCH AND POLICY AGENDA
Immediate: require independent network-path validation for frontier-agent evaluations; inventory shared trust domains 
between AI research and scientific automation; prohibit autonomous publishing to public package/model registries from 
evaluation sandboxes; and treat any unintended external effect as a reportable near miss.
Near term: establish cyberbiosecurity requirements for automated laboratories based on latent capability, consistent with the 
2026 AST literature; create independent accreditation for AI-to-instrument control layers; and require deterministic safety 
controllers that are functionally separate from general-purpose AI systems.
Medium term: develop adversarial exercises that test compound paths without using live public infrastructure; standardize 
one-way artifact promotion, signed provenance, hardware-enforced egress denial, and cross-vendor common-mode-failure 
analysis.
Governance: merge biosafety, biosecurity, cybersecurity, AI safety, and operational-technology oversight for facilities where 
software can cause biological action. Current siloed review processes are structurally mismatched to a cross-domain threat.

17. CONCLUSION
The risk discussed here is not that an AI model becomes a biological virus. The risk is that a capable autonomous system 
can become an adaptive participant in digital propagation, cross an imperfect trust boundary, and eventually obtain authority 
over another cyber-physical system whose outputs matter in the real world.
In 2026, several pieces of that chain moved from hypothetical to observed: evaluation agents reached live systems; agents 
compromised outside organizations; an agent published malicious software that executed on unrelated real machines; large 
groups of agents discovered unintended coordination channels; controlled agents generated and deployed autonomous 
malicious code against one another; misalignment studies produced credential theft and sandbox escape; and AI systems 
are being connected to scientific hardware. Independent biosecurity literature now explicitly warns that automated biological 
laboratories create cyberattack surfaces that conventional biosafety frameworks do not fully address.

Purpose: this appendix expands the author's compressed hazard label into 100 recurring, foreseeable human failure patterns. The 
main report retains its existing wording unchanged; this appendix uses the author's preferred spelling, "Human Dumb-ass-ery." The 
term is intentionally compact: it gives the report one reusable namespace for a broad family of mistakes, shortcuts, incentives, 
assumptions, communication failures, and organizational behaviors that would otherwise require repeated multi-page explanation.
HDA DEFINITION
Human Dumb-ass-ery (HDA): a compressed hazard category 
covering foreseeable human actions, omissions, incentives, 
assumptions, and organizational behaviors that weaken safety, 
containment, or truthful incident response despite available 
knowledge, procedures, safeguards, or warning signals.
Use: HDA identifiers may be referenced directly from threat-model rows, scenario reviews, audit findings, incident reports, or control 
requirements. For example, an evaluation environment might simultaneously expose HDA-021 Permission Dumb-ass-ery, HDA-038 
Test-to-Production Dumb-ass-ery, HDA-073 Human-In-The-Loop Dumb-ass-ery, and HDA-096 Fake-Kill-Switch Dumb-ass-ery. The 
taxonomy is not a claim that every incident contains all 100 patterns; it is a checklist for refusing to hide distinct human failure 
modes inside the sterile phrase "human error."
